What hoobiltit is

The app reads public records. When you drop a pin, it queries map services published by state transportation departments, counties, cities and federal agencies, and shows you what those services return, naming the agency each value came from and when it was fetched. That is the whole of it. There is no server of ours in between, and no judgement of ours added to what the agency said.

What hoobiltit is not

It is not a survey. It is not a title search or a title report. It does not determine boundaries, easements, rights of access, or who holds an interest in land. It is not an engineering assessment or an inspection of a road's condition. It is not legal advice, and using it does not make us your lawyer. It is not a navigation product: it gives no routing, no hazard warnings, no speed limits and no right-of-way information.

It is also not a utility locate. If you are planning to dig, fence, install a driveway or a mailbox, or do anything else in or beside a road, call 811 before you start. Knowing who maintains a road tells you nothing about what is buried under it.

Accuracy, and where its limits are

hoobiltit reports what public agencies publish, names which agency each fact came from, and says so when it does not know. What it does not do is verify those records. It has no means to. Agencies publish records that are incomplete, out of date and sometimes simply wrong, and the app relays them faithfully — which means it relays those faults too.

So we make no representation about the accuracy, completeness or currency of the underlying records, and you agree not to treat the app as the only basis for a decision. Where something matters — a purchase, a claim, a dispute, a repair — confirm it with the agency named on the card before you rely on it. The app shows you which agency to ask, and that is deliberate.

The distinction worth holding on to is this: what the app gives you is a reading of a public record, not a determination of a fact about the world. "Maintained by Maricopa County Department of Transportation" means MCDOT's published road inventory listed this segment as county-maintained when the app asked. It does not mean a court, a title company, a surveyor, or the county itself would reach that conclusion today.

What particular answers mean

That distinction matters most in a few places, so they are worth spelling out.

Who maintains a road is a conclusion drawn from an agency's own classification of its own roads. It is not a field from a deed and it is not a statement about who is liable for the road's condition, who must repair it, or who must clear it.

"Privately owned" means an agency's record classifies the road that way. "Not publicly maintained" means something weaker and more easily misread: no agency the app can query lists the segment as maintained. It is an inference from the absence of a record, and the app marks it as inferred. Neither answer determines that a road is in fact private, that nobody owes a duty to maintain or clear it, that you may or may not use it, or that anything follows for the value of adjoining property.

Dates are the agency's recorded dates, and they are different facts that the app keeps apart rather than merging: when a road was constructed, when a contract was let, and when it was last improved are three different things, and a contract let date is not the date work finished.

Costs, where shown, are usually the agency's estimate rather than what was finally awarded or paid. The app says which it has.

Parcel information comes from assessor records kept for taxation. A parcel outline is not a boundary survey, and the owner of a parcel beside a road is not the owner of the road.

Coverage varies, and a gap is not evidence. Where the app shows nothing, that means no agency it can reach published the answer — not that the answer does not exist.
